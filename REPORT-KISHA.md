## The Artefact

CarFun is a carpooling web application built by five Bath Spa University RAK students to serve the commuting needs of the student community at Bath Spa Academic Centre RAK in Ras Al Khaimah, UAE. At its core it does one thing: it connects students who live in the same parts of RAK and are making the same daily journey to campus, so they can share the ride and share the cost instead of each doing it alone.

The application is fully functional. Students can register with their residential area and commute schedule, browse upcoming rides filtered by area and date, request a seat on a driver's ride, accept or decline requests, chat with matched riders through an in-app messaging system, receive real-time notifications when their request is accepted or a new message arrives, and pin their precise pickup location on a map so the driver knows exactly where to collect them. Drivers manage all of this from a personal dashboard. An admin panel handles user-level management.

Our five-person crew divided the work by discipline. Dhovie Bryan Costimiano and Fareedi Danish Baloch handled the full technical build of the application. Geof Raneses and I, Kisha Romeo, led the marketing strategy and research workstream, which covered the audience analysis, competitive landscape, social media strategy, and the commercial argument for the product. Anas Imtiaz was responsible for media production, including the visual design and video content.

The campaign materials include a full set of Instagram posts and stories, TikTok video scripts, and caption and hashtag libraries, all stored in the project's `marketing/` folder.

CarFun aligns strongly with the incubator theme. It targets students, a group that has real financial constraints and a genuine unmet need around daily transport. It reduces a recurring, unavoidable expense. And it contributes to environmental sustainability by reducing the number of single-occupancy car journeys made each day on the same route. The UN's Sustainable Development Goal 11 calls for sustainable, safe, and inclusive transport systems, and a tool that increases vehicle occupancy among a regular commuter group is a direct practical contribution to that (United Nations, 2015). CarFun also has a clear commercial structure, with a path to revenue through service fees, which means it is not a charity project but a sustainable business model that benefits students, drivers, and the platform.

## The Development

### Ideation and Planning

Coming into this module I knew I wanted our crew to build something genuinely useful rather than something that only worked in theory. The brief gave us a lot of freedom, which meant the hardest part of the start was not the technical work but the focus. We needed to land on a problem that was real, that we could actually solve in the time we had, and that had a clear audience.

The commuting problem came up naturally in our early conversations. It was something everyone in the team understood from personal experience. Students from Al Nakheel, Al Hamra, Khuzam, and other areas all making the same trip to campus every day, most of them alone, most of them spending more than they needed to. The informal coordination that was already happening in group chats told us the need was real. What was missing was the platform to make it reliable.

Once we committed to the idea, Geof and I took on the research phase. I was specifically focused on the audience side: who these students were, what their actual behaviour looked like, what would make them trust and adopt a new product. Geof led on the competitive analysis and the commercial framing. Together we produced a research brief that shaped the product decisions the development team made throughout the build.

### My Contribution

My work on this project falls into two main areas: audience research and social media content strategy.

**Audience Research**

I spoke to a number of BathSpa RAK students about their commute in the weeks before development began. These were not structured academic interviews but genuinely informal conversations, which I think produced more honest answers. I asked how they currently got to campus, how much it cost them on average per month, whether they had ever shared a ride and what that experience was like, and what would make them trust an app like CarFun enough to use it regularly.

A few things came out of those conversations that shaped the product significantly.

The first was that the financial pressure was real and consistent. Almost everyone I spoke to mentioned transport cost when I asked about what stressed them financially at university. The figure of AED 260 or more per month for solo commuting came up multiple times. This gave us the savings number that became central to the marketing messaging: by splitting costs, the average user could save around AED 65 per month. That number is based on real conversations, not an invented statistic.

The second was that trust was the barrier, not behaviour. Students were not opposed to carpooling. They were uncertain about doing it with people they did not already know well. The driver rating system that the development team built into the app was a direct response to this. Visible ratings give passengers some assurance about who they are getting into a car with, which lowers the trust barrier enough to make a first request feel less risky.

The third was that reliability was as important as cost. Students told me that informal WhatsApp-based coordination had let them down before. A driver cancels last minute, a message gets missed, the group chat goes quiet. They wanted something more dependable. This reinforced the case for a platform with clear ride statuses, a request and confirmation flow, and real-time notifications when things changed.

I kept notes from all of these conversations in my learning journal throughout the module, which also tracked how the research findings were being reflected in product decisions during our team meetings. Being able to point to specific student quotes when we were debating features made those discussions more grounded and faster to resolve.

**Social Media Content Strategy**

With the research done, I developed the social media content strategy for CarFun alongside Geof. My focus was on the content itself: the specific posts, the messaging for each one, the tone of voice, and how each piece of content would connect with a student who was seeing CarFun for the first time.

I built the content strategy around three stages of the awareness journey. The first stage is discovery: the student does not know CarFun exists. The content here needs to catch attention and create curiosity. The second stage is consideration: the student is aware of CarFun but has not signed up. The content here needs to explain the value clearly and remove friction. The third stage is conversion: the student is ready to join. The content here needs a clear, direct call to action.

The five Instagram post concepts map onto these stages. The hero brand post is a discovery piece. The how it works post is a consideration piece. The savings post is consideration and conversion. The driver recruitment post is a targeted conversion piece for a specific sub-audience. The community and social proof post is conversion through trust-building.

For TikTok, I developed the narrative structure of each script to follow a format that performs well on the platform. Every script opens with a hook that speaks directly to a pain point the target user has. It then moves quickly to the problem framing, the product introduction, and a call to action. The language in each script is deliberately conversational because TikTok rewards authenticity. Scripts that sound like they were written to be read aloud in a lecture do not land on that platform.

I briefed the hashtag strategy to cover three tiers: broad reach tags, mid-tier community tags, and product-specific tags. Using all three in every post gives each piece of content the best possible chance of being discovered by users at different levels of familiarity with the topic.

I worked closely with Anas throughout this process so that the visual direction of the assets matched the content strategy. It is not enough for the copy to be strong if the visuals communicate something different. Consistency between message and image is what makes a campaign feel coherent rather than cobbled together.

## Routes to Market

### Target Audience

The primary users are Bath Spa RAK students who commute regularly from residential areas across Ras Al Khaimah. Based on the research Geof and I conducted, the areas with the highest density of commuting students include Al Nakheel, Al Hamra, Khuzam, and Al Marjan Island, though students from all the major RAK areas are represented in the campus population.

Within the broader audience, the two sub-segments with the strongest motivation to use CarFun immediately are students paying for daily ride-hailing services, who have an obvious and direct financial incentive, and students who drive alone and would welcome consistent passengers to offset fuel costs.

The secondary audience worth targeting in the medium term is the wider BathSpa RAK student community, including first-year students who are still figuring out their commute and have not yet settled into a routine. Catching them early with a clear, trustworthy option is easier than converting students who have already established habits around solo commuting.

### Market Context

The carpooling market globally is large and growing, valued at approximately USD 185 billion in 2023 with consistent projected growth (Statista, 2024). Within that market, the university carpooling niche is genuinely underserved. Existing platforms are designed for longer, less predictable journeys between people who are strangers. They are not well suited to a small, known community making the same regular trip.

Student financial wellbeing data supports the commercial case. A 2022 NUS study found that over 60% of university students in the UK ranked transport among their top three financial concerns (NUS, 2022). The pattern is even more pronounced in car-dependent environments like the UAE where public transport alternatives are limited.

The research I conducted adds a layer of local evidence on top of those broader statistics, confirming that the financial pressure is felt concretely by BathSpa RAK students and that the appetite for a carpooling solution exists.

### Monetisation

The platform is free at launch. This is the right decision because the early goal is trust and adoption, not revenue. Once the community is established, a service fee on accepted ride contributions is the most natural monetisation path. The fee can start small and be introduced transparently, framed as what keeps the platform running rather than as a cost imposed on users.

Local business partnerships with student-relevant brands offer a secondary revenue stream that does not require users to pay anything. These could include campus-adjacent food outlets, bookshops, or student services providers who want to reach the BathSpa RAK community specifically.

A university partnership is also worth pursuing. If Bath Spa University endorses or partially funds CarFun as part of its sustainability commitments, it adds institutional credibility that accelerates adoption among students who might otherwise be cautious about a new platform.

## Evaluation

### Project and Digital Campaign

The prototype is strong. The development team delivered a working application that covers every key user need our research identified. The campaign materials are visually polished and strategically grounded. Looking at everything together, the product and the campaign tell the same story, which is the most important thing a brand launch can achieve.

What I would develop further in the campaign is the evidence layer. We built the initial content around the CarFun story and value proposition, but the most persuasive content for a student audience is other students talking about their experience. A second phase of social media content built around real user testimonials, driver stories, and month-on-month savings updates would build trust faster and more durably than any brand-produced content.

The TikTok scripts are ready to be produced. What they need is filming. Anas has the skills to produce those videos, and the scripts are written to be achievable without specialist equipment. Getting them filmed and live would be the single highest-impact next step for the campaign.

### Team Collaboration

Our team worked well because everyone took their role seriously. This sounds obvious but it is not guaranteed. In group projects it is common for one or two people to carry the weight while others coast. That did not happen here. The research Geof and I did was real research, not a formality. The development Dhovie and Fareedi produced was a real working product, not a mockup. Anas's media work produced real assets, not placeholder concepts.

The tension we had was around timing. Marketing planning and development run at different paces, and there were moments where the campaign was ready to move before the product features it was promoting were finalized. We handled this by keeping the campaign plan flexible and maintaining communication with the developers on where things were. The final two weeks were the most coordinated phase of the project, and the daily check-in format we used then would have been worth starting sooner.

### Individual Development

This project gave me a much clearer sense of what research is actually for. I have done research for academic assignments before and the experience of doing it for a product that real people would use was completely different. The findings had direct consequences. Students told us trust was a barrier and the app got a rating system. Students told us reliability was the issue and the app got real-time notifications. Seeing that link between what I found and what got built made the research feel like it had real weight.

On the content strategy side, I found that the discipline of working through audience stages before writing any copy made the content sharper. In past projects I would have gone straight to ideas. Working from the framework of discovery, consideration, and conversion first gave every piece of content a clear job to do, and that clarity shows in the work.

Where I want to improve is in the briefing process. My ideas were clear in my head and in conversation with Geof and Anas, but translating them into written briefs was something I left informal. A more structured briefing document for each asset would have made the execution process smoother and the review process easier. That is a habit I want to build in future projects regardless of team size.

CarFun is a product I believe has a real future at BathSpa RAK. The problem is genuine, the solution is functional, and the community it is built for is already motivated. With the right campaign push and a bit more development polish, it is the kind of tool that could become a normal part of how students talk about getting to campus.
